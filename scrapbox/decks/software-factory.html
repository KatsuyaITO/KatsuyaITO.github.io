1

Define: what a software factory is, and what it is not

One definition, two births of the word, and four neighbours it gets confused with: Claude Code, Ralph loops, CI/CD and "dark" automation.

Part 1 sets shared vocabulary. The word "software factory" is used loosely on HN and X, and threads like "Personal software factories are a waste of time" (Oct 2026) argue mostly about the definition. So we fix one definition first, then contrast it with the things people mix it up with: a single agent harness like Claude Code, a Ralph-style while-true loop, and ordinary CI/CD and platform engineering. We end with Shapiro's five levels, the light/twilight/dark vocabulary, and why this became possible in 2025–26.
1 · Define · The one-slide definition

A software factory turns intent into verified software with a line of agent loops

Osmani's short form: "an org chart made of loops". The agent is one part. Queue, sandboxes, verification, gates and feedback make it a factory.

Addy Osmani, "Software Factories, Light and Dark" (2026-07) StrongDM, "Software Factories and the Agentic Moment" (2026-02) Dan Shapiro, "The Five Levels" (2026-01) HumanLayer, "Why Software Factories Fail" (2026-07)
Where the definition comes from. There is no standard. The sentence on the title is our synthesis of three sources. Addy Osmani gives the most usable one: many harnessed loops running at once, fed by a queue of work and drained through a review gate into production, with humans above the system owning it. Dan Shapiro's Level 5 calls the end state "a black box that turns specs into software". StrongDM's manifesto (Feb 2026) made the term popular with two rules: humans don't write code and humans don't review code; they verify with holdout scenarios kept outside the repo. Dex Horthy (HumanLayer) grounds the term in the pre-AI 2022 pipeline: decide, tracker, build, PR review, ship, monitor, feedback into the tracker. In the agent version, "build" becomes agents and review becomes the bottleneck. The 11-station version of this line is Part 2. 1 · Define · History

The factory was born twice: 1968 to standardize human work, 2026 to run without it

Osmani (2026-07), on Bemer 1968 Hitachi, software history Cusumano, Japan's Software Factories (1991) Greenfield, "The Case for Software Factories" (2004) Breaking Defense (2022-02) Latent Space, AIEWF dispatch (2026-07)
Same word, different problem. Osmani traces the term to Bob Bemer's 1968 paper "The economics of program production" (we did not read the original). The 1968 NATO conference in Garmisch coined "software engineering", and McIlroy argued for mass-produced, catalogued components. Hitachi's own history says its Software Works became independent in 1969 as the world's first software-dedicated facility; Toshiba, NEC and Fujitsu followed. Cusumano's 1991 book described these as "flexible mass production": standards, reuse, quality control. Microsoft's 2004 Software Factories (Greenfield and Short) aimed at economies of scope with models and DSLs, an ancestor of today's spec-driven development. From 2017, the US Air Force's Kessel Run and later the DoD (29 factories by 2022, per Breaking Defense) used "software factory" for a DevSecOps platform plus in-house agile teams. Every earlier wave moved craft into a system but kept people as the workers. In 2026 the worker becomes an agent, so the question becomes how much can run unattended. Wavect's essay judges that the Japanese factories stayed company-specific wins; the same question applies to AI factories.
1 · Define · Is / is not

A factory is the system around the agents: queue, checks, gates and feedback

A software factory is

  • Many agent loops running at once, fed by a queue
  • A line from intent to production, end to end
  • Machine verification as the main check on every change
  • Humans setting intent and owning outcomes and risk
  • A feedback loop from production back into specs and context

It is not

  • One smarter agent, a bigger prompt, or one long chat
  • A while true loop on its own
  • CI/CD with a chatbot added
  • "No humans" by definition: dark is one setting of it
  • Proof of quality: green tests can hide unreadable code

Test for any claimed factory: who decides a change is done, and what happens when that check is wrong?

Osmani, "Software Factories, Light and Dark" (2026-07) HumanLayer, "Why Software Factories Fail" (2026-07) Mollick, "Agency and Agents" (2026-08) HN, "Personal software factories are a waste of time" (2026-10)
The left column is the common core across Osmani, StrongDM, Shapiro and Horthy. The right column lists the confusions we saw in the HN threads, where people asked whether a factory is just a bundle of AGENTS.md files or different from Claude Cowork. Osmani is explicit: a factory "is not a bigger agent; it is an org chart made of loops". "No humans" is a design choice, StrongDM's choice, and Mollick argues it is the wrong goal for most organizations. The last row comes from HumanLayer: in July 2025 they went lights-off, and by around the third outage in November a cofounder rewrote the code by hand over two weeks. Their explanation is that RL rewards score pass/fail only, so nothing penalizes eroding maintainability. The question at the bottom is the one to ask any vendor or internal team calling something a factory.
1 · Define · vs Claude Code

Claude Code is one worker on the line; the factory is the plant around it

Spotify kept its Fleet Management pipeline (targeting, PRs, review, merge) and swapped only the transformation step for an agent, later Claude Code.

anthropics/claude-code openai/codex Spotify Engineering, Honk part 1 (2025-11) Honk part 2 (2025-11) Thorsten Ball, "How to Build an Agent" (2025-04)
Tool versus plant. A coding agent is, in Thorsten Ball's words, an LLM, a loop and enough tokens; he builds one in under 400 lines. Claude Code adds tools, subagents, hooks, experimental agent teams, a headless mode and an SDK, which is why it sits inside many factories: Gas Town, Fabro, Ralph loops and Spotify's Honk all run on it. Codex CLI has a non-interactive exec mode and is the worker in OpenAI's Symphony. A factory is everything else: what starts work, where the spec lives, how each agent gets an isolated environment, who checks the result, what merges, and how incidents flow back. Spotify is the cleanest proof that the parts are separable: they kept the plumbing and replaced only the transformation step, after their Maven updater script had grown past 20,000 lines. They moved from Goose, Aider and a homegrown loop to Claude Code without changing the pipeline. The 11 stations here are the blueprint of Part 2; "Orchestr." is orchestration.
1 · Define · vs loop engineering

A Ralph loop repeats one station; a factory wires many loops to specs and gates

Geoffrey Huntley, "Ralph Wiggum as a software engineer" (2025-07) Osmani (2026-07) StrongDM (2026-02) Carlini, Anthropic, "Building a C compiler" (2026-02)
Loop engineering is a building block. Huntley's Ralph, in its purest form, is the Bash loop on the slide: the same prompt every time, a fresh context each run, and state kept on disk in specs, a plan file and git. He does one thing per loop and budgets about 170k tokens of context. It is monolithic on purpose: one process in one repo, with subagents for search and writing but only one running build and test. Huntley calls it deterministically bad in an undeterministic world, with failures fixed by tuning the prompt. Osmani's vocabulary helps: a loop is one agent doing one job on repeat, a harness is the walls around a loop, a factory is many harnessed loops behind a review gate. The factory adds what a single loop lacks: a queue of different tasks, verification the agent cannot game (StrongDM keeps scenarios outside the repo like a holdout set because agents can rewrite tests), a merge gate, and failures that become new work. Carlini's C compiler sits between the two: 16 parallel loops, about 2,000 sessions and under $20,000, coordinated only by lock files in git, with GCC as the near-perfect oracle.

What is actually new. CI/CD, DevOps and platform engineering already gave us the pipeline: tracker, build, PR, review, ship, monitor. The 2019 DoD DevSecOps reference design calls that a software factory. Agents plug into the same pipeline, so the plumbing is old. What changes is who sits in the build seat. 1 · Define · Shapiro's Five Levels

Most company factories sit at Level 3: agents write code, humans still read diffs

Dan Shapiro, "The Five Levels" (2026-01) Stripe Ramp Uber Spotify OpenAI StrongDM Yegge
The ladder. Dan Shapiro (Glowforge CEO) borrowed the self-driving levels. L0 manual. L1 AI intern: autocomplete and small tasks. L2 junior buddy: pairing, where he puts about 90% of developers. L3: you become a reviewer, "Your life is diffs", and almost everyone tops out here. L4: you write specs and skills, leave for hours, then check whether tests pass; he says he is here. L5 borrows "dark factory" from FANUC's robot-staffed plant: a black box that turns specs into software; he says only a few teams of under five people work there. Placement is our reading. Stripe merges over 1,000 minion-produced PRs a week but still human-reviews them, so L3. Ramp's PRs are opened with the user's own token so nobody approves their own agent's change. Uber attributes over 70% of PRs to agents. Spotify Honk and OpenAI's harness work are spec- and verifier-driven with humans checking outcomes, so L4; OpenAI's team says no line of the ~1M-line product was written by hand. StrongDM's rules are no human writes or reviews code. Yegge says Gas Town is 100% vibe coded and he does not look at the code, so we put it at L5, though his later Gas City post says it keeps the lights on. Carlini's compiler ran 16 agents with a GCC oracle and he says he is uneasy deploying code nobody verified.
1 · Define · Light · Twilight · Dark

Light, twilight or dark is set per loop, by how cheaply its output can be checked

HumanLayer, "Why Software Factories Fail" (2026-07) Mollick, "Agency and Agents" (2026-08) StrongDM (2026-02) Osmani (2026-07) Shapiro (2026-01)
The vocabulary. "Dark factory" is a manufacturing term: a plant where machines do so much that you can turn off the lights; Shapiro cites FANUC. StrongDM is the radical primary example: humans neither write nor review code; holdout scenarios, a probabilistic "satisfaction" score and a Digital Twin Universe of cloned services (Okta, Jira, Slack) do the checking, and they say spending under $1,000 of tokens per engineer per day means room for improvement. Dex Horthy's essay says plainly that the lights-off factory does not work: HumanLayer went full lights-off in July 2025 and by about the third outage in November a cofounder rewrote it by hand; his section heading is "Turning the lights back on", and he aims for a safe 2–3x instead of 10–100x. Mollick's "Twilight Factory" sits between: agents do most of the work but proactively reach out to humans, with a facilitator agent deciding when. Osmani turns this into a design rule per loop, and warns that going dark builds comprehension debt while tests stay green. Yegge's later Gas City post also says it keeps the lights on and maximizes observability. Part 4 returns to this debate.

2 · Blueprint · The whole line

A factory is nine stations in a line, plus a feedback loop and governance

2 · Blueprint · Our reading of 14 cases

Higher levels pull humans out of review; at L5 they keep only intake and spec

Shapiro, "The Five Levels" (2026-01) Stripe, "Minions" (2026-02) OpenAI, "Harness engineering" (2026-02) StrongDM, "Software Factories and the Agentic Moment" (2026-02) Spotify, "Honk, Part 1" (2025-11)
Method. For each of 14 cases we drew a swimlane from the public write-up and marked who runs each station: human, agent, machine, or not described. Placing a company on a level is our reading, not their claim. L3 (Stripe, Ramp, Dropbox, Uber, Cloudflare, HumanLayer): humans start every run and approve every merge. 2 · Blueprint · Stations 01–02

Requests enter as tickets and leave as written specs the agent follows

What it does

Turns requests into small tasks, then writes what to build, and how, as files.

Key finding

Thoughtworks sorts tools into spec-first, spec-anchored and spec-as-source; heavy spec tools are overkill for small fixes.

Open question

How detailed? Too thin and agents guess; too thick and it is just slower code.

Stripe, "Minions" (2026-02) OpenAI, symphony (2026-02) Datadog, Bits AI Dev Agent docs GitHub, spec-kit (2025-08) Böckeler, "Understanding Spec-Driven Development" (2025-10)
Intake. Four common triggers: tag a bot in Slack and get a PR back (Stripe Minions, Ramp Inspect); an orchestrator watches a Linear board and starts one run per ticket (OpenAI Symphony); production errors open draft PRs with no human request (Datadog Bits, which still never auto-merges); a platform team defines one change and fans it out to many repos (Spotify Fleetshift, Uber). The pre-AI idea here is Toyota pull: make only what is needed, so work is not pushed in blindly. Spec. Spec Kit runs constitution, specify, plan, tasks, implement. Kiro is an IDE built on requirements (EARS notation), design and tasks. HumanLayer ACE is research, plan, implement, with a human reviewing the plan. StrongDM publishes the natural-language spec itself and lets any agent build it. Böckeler (Thoughtworks) warns that spec-as-source risks combining the flaws of model-driven development and LLMs. Open problem: splitting messy human requests into agent-sized tasks is still mostly manual.
2 · Blueprint · Station 03

Context is a budget: a short map always, everything else on demand

What it does

Gives the agent what a new hire would need, loaded only when relevant.

Key finding

OpenAI: give Codex "a map, not a 1,000-page instruction manual"; the docs folder is the source of truth.

Open question

Docs and AGENTS.md drift from the code, and nobody can cheaply tell which context is stale.

OpenAI, "Harness engineering" (2026-02) Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents" Uber, AI Engineer talk (2026-06) Uber, "Efficient software factory" (2026-08) HumanLayer, ACE (2025)
Four layers, from always-on to on-demand. OpenAI keeps AGENTS.md to about 100 lines that point into a structured docs/ directory, kept fresh by linters, CI and a doc-gardening agent. Mitchell Hashimoto adds a line to AGENTS.md each time an agent makes a mistake. Cloudflare generated short AGENTS.md files for about 3,900 repos. Tools come through MCP gateways: Stripe Toolshed has about 500 tools, Uber reports 1,000+ behind one gateway. Skills are folders of instructions and scripts loaded only when needed; Uber reports 3,600+. The gauge is the HumanLayer ACE rule of thumb: keep context use around 40 to 60 percent and compact often. Huntley makes the same point for Ralph: the less of the window you use, the better the result. Anthropic frames the window as a scarce budget, filled by just-in-time retrieval, compaction and notes. Open problem: context drifts from code silently.
2 · Blueprint · Station 04

Orchestration runs many agents at once, but human attention does not scale

What it does

Splits work into a task graph and keeps agents running on it.

Key finding

Osmani, "The Orchestration Tax": adding agents is easy; human attention does not parallelize, so it becomes the bottleneck.

Open question

How much control flow to give the model versus fixing it in code is still taste, not evidence.

Huntley, "Ralph Wiggum as a software engineer" (2025-07) gastownhall/gastown Stripe, "Minions, Part 2" strongdm/attractor (2026-02) Carlini, "Building a C compiler" (2026-02) Osmani, "The Orchestration Tax" (2026-05)
Three shapes. The Ralph loop is a bash while loop that feeds the same prompt to the agent again and again; state lives in the spec, the plan and git, not in the context window. Huntley says it is for greenfield code only. The coordinator shape is Gas Town: a Mayor agent reads workers' output and talks to you, and tasks live in Beads, a git-backed dependency graph. Claude Code subagents and agent teams use a lead agent with a shared task list. The graph runner puts control flow in code or a DOT graph; Stripe Blueprints mix deterministic nodes (lint, push) with agent nodes (implement, fix CI), and StrongDM Attractor runs a Graphviz pipeline. A fourth, very common shape is kanban plus worktrees: one card, one worktree, one agent, the human manages the board (Vibe Kanban, Conductor). Carlini ran 16 parallel agents with no orchestrator, claiming tasks by writing lock files. Anthropic's "Building effective agents" advises the simplest pattern that works.
2 · Blueprint · Station 05

Each agent gets its own disposable dev environment, walled off from production

What it does

Gives each agent a safe copy of the dev environment, plus fakes of outside services.

Key finding

Stripe names the key trait as "the absence of a supervisory human": isolation is what makes that safe.

Open question

Keeping sandboxes realistic (data, services, secrets) without making them dangerous or costly.

Stripe, "Minions, Part 2" Ramp, "Why we built our background agent" (2026-01) Modal, "How Ramp built…" (2026-02) StrongDM, Digital Twin Universe Agache et al., "Firecracker" (NSDI 2020)
Why isolation comes first. Without it you either approve every command, or risk an agent touching production and secrets. With it, Stripe runs agents with full permissions and no confirmation prompts inside pre-warmed devboxes that start in about 10 seconds and are cut off from production and the internet. Ramp Inspect runs on Modal: a VM with Postgres, Redis, Temporal, RabbitMQ, a browser and VNC, from a repo image rebuilt every 30 minutes, so the agent can check its own UI with screenshots. StrongDM built behavioral clones of six outside services (Okta, Jira, Slack, Google apps and others), refined against the real ones "until we stop finding behavioral differences." At the cheap end, git worktrees give one folder and branch per agent on a laptop (Conductor). At the strong end, Firecracker microVMs from AWS start fast and isolate strongly; many sandbox products build on them. Jake Saunders shows a small self-hosted version that needs no platform team.
2 · Blueprint · Station 06

The worker is a model in a loop with tools, and the tools shape its skill

What it does

The coding agent itself: a model that reads, edits, runs and tests code in a loop.

Key finding

SWE-agent (Princeton): designing the agent's commands for search, view and edit raised results without changing the model.

Open question

Training rewards tests passing; "there is no penalty for eroding codebase maintainability" (HumanLayer).

Yang et al., "SWE-agent" (2024-05) Ball, "How to Build an Agent" HumanLayer, "Why Software Factories Fail" (2026-07) block goose OpenHands
The harness is thinner than people expect. Thorsten Ball (Amp) builds a working agent in under 400 lines: a model, a loop and a few tools. mini-swe-agent does it in about 100 lines with bash as the only tool, a useful baseline. Most factories put an off-the-shelf harness at this station: Claude Code or its Agent SDK (headless mode, hooks, subagents), Codex CLI (the worker in OpenAI Symphony), goose (Stripe Minions run a fork), OpenHands. Spotify moved from Goose, Aider and a homegrown loop to Claude Code. SWE-agent coined the agent-computer interface: the shape of the commands and their output matters, not only the model. OpenAI's "harness engineering" widens the term to linters and structural tests around the agent. The open problem is in the model, not the harness: HumanLayer argues RL rewards and benchmarks score pass or fail only, so models drift toward code that passes but is hard to maintain, and there is no fast oracle for maintainability yet.
2 · Blueprint · Station 07

Agents solve whatever the verifier checks, so stack checks up to hard-to-game

What it does

Checks the agent's work by machine before any human looks at it.

Key finding

Carlini (Anthropic): make sure "the task verifier is nearly perfect, otherwise Claude will solve the wrong problem."

Open question

Tests prove behavior, not maintainability or intent, and an LLM judge can share the agent's blind spots.

Carlini, "Building a C compiler" (2026-02) StrongDM, factory (2026-02) Spotify, "Honk, Part 3" (2025-12) Stripe, "Minions" (2026-02) OpenAI, symphony Petrović, Ivanković, "Mutation Testing at Google" (2018)
Read the stack bottom up. Formatters, linters and type checks are cheap and run on every change. Tests and CI come next; Stripe lets the agent fix CI failures but caps it at two rounds, because returns diminish and tokens cost money. Spotify Honk runs verifiers in a stop hook before any PR and then an LLM judge compares the diff to the task; Spotify reported the judge vetoed about a quarter of sessions, but we could not verify that figure, and a later report says the judge was removed. StrongDM calls them scenarios, not tests, because agents can rewrite tests to match the code; scenarios live outside the repo like a holdout set and give a probabilistic satisfaction score. OpenAI Symphony asks for proof of work on every PR: CI results, review notes, a video. Carlini used GCC as a known-good oracle for his C compiler, and wrote test output for the agent: a few lines on screen, details in grep-able log files. Pre-AI ideas that apply: mutation testing (test your tests) and Toyota jidoka (stop on an abnormality so defects do not flow downstream). Cognition's Frontier Code benchmark tries to measure whether a maintainer would merge, beyond pass or fail.
2 · Blueprint · Station 08

Review is the new bottleneck, and teams split on who holds the gate

What it does

Decides which changes merge, with stricter rules for riskier code.

Key finding

Google's report on AI-driven code migrations finds human review becomes the bottleneck even when generation works.

Open question

AI reviewers raise the floor, not the ceiling (HumanLayer). No review UX fits agent volume yet.

Nikolov et al., "How is Google using AI for internal code migrations?" (2025-01) Cloudflare, "Internal AI engineering stack" (2026-04) Stripe, "Minions" (2026-02) Ramp, background agent (2026-01) Osmani, "Light and Dark" (2026-07) Faros AI, "AI acceleration whiplash"
Three positions on the gate. Stripe merges over 1,000 fully agent-written PRs a week, and every one is still human-reviewed; Google and Datadog also keep human approval and no auto-merge. Cloudflare runs an AI code reviewer on 100% of repos on its standard CI: it sorts each merge request into trivial, lite or full risk and sends it to specialist agents for quality, security, docs and performance. Uber scores its uReview on precision and recall against real PRs with known bugs. StrongDM is the dark end: humans neither write nor review code, and trust comes from holdout scenarios. Ryan Carson's Code Factory recipe chooses checks by risk and requires machine-checkable evidence. Osmani warns that going dark builds comprehension debt while tests stay green, and suggests keeping humans on auth, billing and public APIs. Faros AI reports that when review is skipped under AI volume, incidents per PR rise sharply (their figure is +242.7%, with 31.3% of PRs merged without review); we could not verify these numbers ourselves.
2 · Blueprint · Stations 09–10

Ship with a small blast radius, and feed every failure back into the factory

What it does

Ships merged changes safely, watches production, and routes failures back upstream.

Key finding

DORA 2025: AI use goes with higher throughput but lower delivery stability.

Open question

Who is on call for code no human wrote or read, and when should an agent stop the line?

Google SRE Workbook, "Canarying releases" Majors, "Production Is Where the Rigor Goes" (2026-03) DORA 2025 report (2025-09) Datadog, Bits AI Dev Agent Hashimoto, "My AI adoption journey" (2026-02) LEI, "Andon"
Ship. At ten times the change volume, production is where bad changes show up first, so blast radius must be small: merge behind a feature flag (Ramp uses LaunchDarkly), send a small share of traffic to the new version, compare, then widen (Google SRE canarying). Charity Majors argues that at 10 to 100x change rates, rigor has to move into production observability; telemetry is the spec that survived. Birgitta Böckeler of Thoughtworks sorts spec-driven development into three levels. Spec-first: a well-thought-out spec is written, then used for the task. Spec-anchored: the spec is kept after the task and used to evolve the feature. Spec-as-source: the spec is the main source file and the human never touches the code. She places Kiro and Spec Kit at spec-first (GitHub aspires to anchoring, but each spec lives on its own branch), and Tessl as the only one explicitly aiming at spec-anchored and exploring spec-as-source. 4 · Debates · History

Earlier software factories raised quality but stalled; Toyota's stop-the-line rule is worth borrowing

The "factory" label has been tried at least four times before AI. The optimistic reading is that each wave moved craft into systems and AI is the fourth reinterpretation; the pessimistic one is that AI factories may also end as internal efficiency. The bottom row is our angle: our survey found English-language essays that cite FANUC's lights-out plant but none that connect AI factories to Toyota's jidoka and andon, where the line stops on a defect and a person is called. 5 · The stack · Verify

Human-paced CI assumes a few pushes per person per day and a person who reads the failure. Our view: tacit knowledge decides whether a factory pays

Parts 1 to 5 reported what others say. This part is our own opinion, with research added only where it supports a point.

From here on this is the presenter's own view, not a survey. Six points: (1) most customer knowledge is tacit, (2) build factories as lanes that grow with demand, (3) small drift compounds, so correct on a cadence, (4) shared documents act as a common language, (5) whether a factory makes money depends on where the bottleneck moves, (6) the cornerstones we need. Where a slide cites research, the source line says so; everything else is our opinion.
6 · Our view · Tacit knowledge

About 80% of what a customer knows is below the waterline, out of the factory's reach

Our view (presenter's opinion)
A factory can build "an attendance clock-in button" or "a CRM" if you ask for it. But each customer carries a large amount of tacit knowledge: who uses the system, what kind of customers they serve, what data exists. Some of it is written down in manuals or already sits in data. Much more of it is only on site: who actually uses it, what they struggle with every day, how they think while using it, which tasks are most frequent, what they talk about with their own customers. It feels like an 80/20 rule: roughly 80% is buried, and the documented part is only the tip of the iceberg. The 80/20 split is an intuition, not a measured number.
6 · Our view · Tacit knowledge

The factory receives the request, not the context behind it, so it builds a generic answer

Our view HumanLayer, "Why Software Factories Fail" (2026-07)
The factory only sees what passes through the narrow pipe: the request and whatever documents exist. Everything the customer knows but never wrote down is lost on the way in, so the factory builds a technically working but generic answer. Supporting research: HumanLayer's prescription starts with a product review, a short document on the user problem and the success signal with rough HTML mockups of the screens, before any architecture or code. We read that as one way of widening the pipe. The diagram itself is our view.
6 · Our view · How to build one

Build the factory as specialized lanes, adding a lane when demand appears, so each person's load stays flat

Our view Tessl, "Build your software factory, one workflow at a time" (2026-06)
The more you use a factory, the more kinds of tasks it has to handle. Instead of one lane that does everything, build specialized lanes: a frontend lane with its own design and engineer agents, a backend lane with its own lane owner, and so on, adding a new lane only when demand for it shows up. Each lane has one human owner, so the number of people grows with the lanes and no single person has to hold the whole factory in their head. The goal is software engineering that keeps human cognitive load low. Supporting framing: the vendor Tessl markets "build your software factory, one workflow at a time", which matches the incremental idea; we have not evaluated their product.
6 · Our view · Drift

A 1% gap in understanding compounds the longer the factory runs unattended

Our view HumanLayer, "Why Software Factories Fail" (2026-07) Addy Osmani, "Software Factories, Light and Dark" (2026-07)
Small tacit-knowledge gaps, say 1%, grow as automation runs. The further the factory runs unattended, the harder it is to come back, and AI-specific slop accumulates. The curve is arithmetic, not data: 1.01 to the 200th is about 7.3, while resetting every 25 steps keeps the gap near 1.28. Supporting research: HumanLayer went fully lights-off in July 2025 and, by their own account, hit outages from code nobody could read and rewrote by hand in November; they argue training rewards pass/fail with "no penalty for eroding codebase maintainability". Addy Osmani calls the same effect "comprehension debt" when a factory goes dark. Both are self-reported or opinion pieces.
6 · Our view · Drift

So review, develop and get feedback on a fixed cadence, and stop the line when something looks wrong

Our view Lean Enterprise Institute, "Jidoka" Lean Enterprise Institute, "Andon" OpenHands, "What Is An AI Dark Factory?" (2026-09)
Because drift compounds, the answer is regular correction: review regularly, develop regularly, get feedback regularly, on a cadence rather than when someone remembers. Supporting idea from manufacturing: Toyota's jidoka means a machine stops itself on an abnormality so defects never flow downstream, and the andon is the signal to stop and call for help at once. In a software factory, that maps to an agent stopping and escalating to a human instead of building on top of a wrong assumption. Our survey found that the 2026 English-language debate cites lights-out plants like FANUC (for example OpenHands' dark-factory post) but almost never Toyota's jidoka or andon, so this is an angle a Japanese team can contribute.
6 · Our view · Shared language

We use three reviewed documents as the common language: UX flow, design doc, implementation plan

Our view HumanLayer, "Why Software Factories Fail" (2026-07)
Our current practice: three document types, each reviewed before the next is written. The UX flow fixes who does what and in what order; the design doc fixes how the system works; the implementation plan fixes the steps the agents will follow. They lower cognitive load because a reviewer only has to judge one layer at a time, and they become the common language between people and agents. Supporting research: HumanLayer arrived at a similar staged sequence after their lights-off failure: product review, system architecture, program design with call-stack trees and type signatures, then vertical slices of 1 to 3 at a time. They also triage by size; about 40% of tasks are one-shot without the full sequence. HumanLayer sells tooling for this approach.

6 · Our view · Shared language

Reviewing hard is not enough: review has to bridge human tacit knowledge and AI tacit knowledge

Reviewing more and harder is not sufficient on its own. Review is where the two meet, and it works best over shared documents where each side writes down what it assumed. The examples inside the circles are our interpretation of "AI tacit knowledge". Supporting research: HumanLayer's program-design step has the model draft and the human argue with it, and their closing advice is "Read the dang code".

6 · Our view · Does it pay?

When coding becomes easy, the bottleneck moves to selling, adopting and rolling out

Our view Spotify, "Coding Is No Longer the Constraint" (2026-06) peterp, "Personal software factories are a waste of time" (2026-10)
In the end, a software factory is a bottleneck shift. When coding becomes easy, the constraint moves to sales, customer success, planning and consulting. For enterprise: sales, internal approval (the Japanese ringi process), embedding the system in the organization, and rollout on site. For SMB: sales, marketing, price competition and differentiation. In Japan in particular: onboarding, customer success, and evangelism-style marketing and branding. Supporting research: Spotify's 2026 talk says coding is no longer the constraint, with the constraint moving to human decisions on what to build and review (we have not read that page in full). In the HN discussion of peterp's October 2026 post, commenters noted that distribution, not building, is now scarce. The segment lists are the presenter's view.
6 · Our view · Does it pay?

Our open question: go all in on brand, or keep following wherever the bottleneck moves?

Our view (open question, no external source)
If coding becomes cheap for everyone, the question is where to put our effort. Option A is to go all in on branding. Option B is to keep following where scarcity and the bottleneck move: sales, onboarding, customer success, and whatever comes after. The presenter has not decided; this is posed as an open question for discussion, and we come back to it in the discussion questions at the end.
6 · Our view · Cornerstones

The right app for every department needs four cornerstones, built on a factory that keeps its context

Our view (no external source)
The long-term question: if we build a factory where context accumulates the more it builds, can AI eventually make the right application, accurately, for every department? To get there we need four cornerstones. First, context itself, collected and kept. Second, the ability to design software factories. Third, the ability to grasp what customers actually mean, which is the iceberg problem again. Fourth, the ability to turn what we learn into templates and roll them out to the next department or customer.
